## Explanation

Business process management has gone through distinct phases, each with its own ideas about how to improve work. This article covers the early phase and one later landmark.

**Early phase: reengineering.** In 1990, Hammer published an article in the Harvard Business Review (HBR). It argued that managers should remove work that adds no value. Automating that work was the wrong move.

One widely cited definition of reengineering is the fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of business processes.

**Criticism.** Reengineering drew sharp objections:

- Critics said many companies used it as a pretext for downsizing. The label BPR (business process reengineering) became tied to workforce reductions.
- Reported failure rates were high. One figure says 50-70% of projects either failed or brought no significant benefit.
- Davenport said that when he wrote about process redesign in 1990, he explicitly stated that using it for cost reduction alone was not a sensible goal.

**A later landmark.** Carr's article "IT Doesn't Matter" appeared in the May 2003 edition of the HBR.

**Why this matters for architecture.** Hammer's 1990 point was that work that adds no value should be removed rather than automated. For how process work is organized, see the BPM Life Cycle article.

## Example

A team plans to automate a monthly report. Before building anything, they ask who reads it. Nobody does. Following Hammer's 1990 argument, they drop the report instead of automating it. They automate only the steps that add value.

## Trade-offs

Radical redesign can remove waste, but it carries risk. Reported failure rates were high, and the approach became linked with job cuts. Davenport had said explicitly in 1990 that cost reduction alone is not a sensible goal.
