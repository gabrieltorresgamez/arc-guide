## Explanation

A strategy map models the way an organization creates value. It puts strategic objectives on one page and links them with arrows.

**The four perspectives**

The standard map groups objectives into four perspectives:

- Financial
- Customer
- Internal Business Processes
- Learning & Growth

One source says a map organizes 12–20 objectives across these four, while another says usually fewer than 20.

**Reading the arrows**

The logic flows from bottom to top. Goals in lower perspectives contribute to goals in higher ones. For example, a learning goal may support a process goal. That goal may support a customer goal, and so on up to the financial goals.

Each arrow is a hypothesis, not a proven fact. The strategic themes are also hypotheses about how the strategy will change the organization's outcomes.

**Customer perspective**

Map what customers want from your business. Do not map what your business wants from them.

**How it fits with other tools**

The map clarifies the logic of the strategy. It then serves as the backbone for the balanced scorecard, which holds measures and targets. It also underpins initiative portfolios and governance.

**Building a map**

1. Pick the strategic themes.
2. For each theme, write one or two short, action-oriented objectives per perspective.
3. Connect the objectives from bottom to top.
4. Check that the total is about 12–20 objectives (one source's guidance).

**Link to IT and architecture**

The map can show how IT maps to business strategy. Research cited by Kaplan and Norton found that only one-third of IT organizations link their planning and budgeting to business strategy. Intangible assets create value only when they are aligned with that strategy.

## Example

A small retailer wants to grow revenue (Financial). Customers want fast delivery (Customer). So the retailer sets an objective to shorten order handling (Internal Business Processes). That needs staff who can use a better order system (Learning & Growth). The arrows run upward from the staff objective to the revenue objective. Each arrow is a guess the retailer checks later.

## Trade-offs

The four standard headings do not fit every organization, although they remain the most common set. Proving that one objective causes another is hard, so treat links as hypotheses. A map can go static if nobody reviews it on a regular schedule. Generic objectives reduce its value.
