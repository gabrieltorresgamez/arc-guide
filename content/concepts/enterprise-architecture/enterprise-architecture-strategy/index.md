## Explanation

Business strategy does not stand still. Markets evolve, competitors act, and new technologies appear. An enterprise therefore cannot transform once and stop. It needs the ability to transform again and again.

Enterprise architecture (see "Enterprise Architecture") done well works as a business transformation practice. The architect's job is to connect things.

**Starting point and direction**

Practical guidance begins with mapping the current state. Next, the team defines what the architecture should look like in the future.

**How architects steer**

Architects guide the choices of others. They do not dictate them. They also measure change and communicate its value, so everyone understands why it matters.

**Why it matters**

Ross, Weill and Robertson argue that the enterprise architecture may matter far more for execution than the strategy itself. For related ideas, see "Enterprise Architecture (Ross/Weill/Robertson definition)" and "Foundation for Execution".

## Example

A retailer sees customers moving to online ordering. The architects first map how ordering, stock and delivery work today. They then describe how these should work in the future. They share this picture with the teams that make the choices, and they track how each change affects the business. When the market shifts again, they update the picture and repeat.

## Trade-offs

There is one tension. Architects guide rather than dictate, so results depend on others understanding the value of each change. Measuring and communicating takes ongoing effort.
